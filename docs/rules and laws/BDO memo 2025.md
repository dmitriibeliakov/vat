## VAT General Rules

Determining the place of a supply in VAT is important because the Directive has not territorially limited the concept of 'entrepreneur'. This means that every entrepreneur in the world can be a taxpayer for Dutch VAT. That means also that a company established in the Netherlands who makes taxable supplies does not necessarily have VAT on the supplies made in the Netherlands.

The place of supply of services is therefore important in order to determine whether a supply falls within the scope of the VAT Directive and the VAT Acts of the local EU countries. If that is the case, it must then be determined Member State to which the tax revenue accrues.

To determine the place of a service for VAT, a distinction must first be made between services provided to private individual or companies. The general rule for the supply of services to companies is that the place of supply is where the customer is established, or where the permanent establishment of this company is located. If the customer is not an entrepreneur, the place of supply is where the trader who supplied the service is domiciled, established or where the fixed from which the service was provided. The place of a supply is as said important in determining where VAT should be levied. Travel related services may have a large array of places where the services are VAT taxable. As a form of simplification a special scheme was made for travel agents or tour operators. These terms have a very specific rule book so I will spend some time below describing these rules. This is especially important to see how other providers of business may be handling things.

## TOMS-Regime in the Netherlands

Articles 306 to 310 of the VAT Directive (Directive 2006/112/EU) provide for a special scheme for travel agents also known as the Travel agent or Tour operator Margin Scheme or TOMS. The Netherlands did not initially apply the scheme as included in the Directive. As of 1 April 2012, after the European Commission had requested a change in regulations, the correct scheme was included in the Dutch VAT Act.

### Scope of the Dutch TOMS-Regime

The Dutch TOMS-regime applies to the activities performed by a taxable person that qualifies as a 'tour operator/travel agent'. To qualify as a tour operator/travel agent in the meaning of the Dutch TOMS-regime, the following conditions need to be met:

* it should regard a taxable person;
* who acts in its **own name** towards the customer / traveler; and
* bundles supplies of services and supplies of goods purchased from other taxable persons / third party service providers to provide a travel package.

In this respect please note that the term "agent" can cause a lot of discussion, because also intermediaries are often referred to as agent therefore in English I like to refer to the term tour operator when a company actually provides the services as described above and (travel) agent if the company does not act as tour operator in this way.

If the above conditions are met, the Dutch TOMS-regime should in principle apply irrespective of the purpose or the length of the trip. More importantly, it does not matter whether the taxable person normally presents itself as a travel organization nor does it matter whether the sale of the travel packages is (not) part of their normal business activities in the Netherlands. Also is not important whether the customer is a business (traveler) or a private individual.

Note that currently the Dutch TOMS regime (found in artt. 28z up to and including 28zg, XVI en XVII Dutch VAT Act) is in line with the EU TOMS regime (artt. 306–310 VAT-Directive).

As a result of the Dutch TOMS-regime, the place of supply of the (travel) services is deemed to be situated in the Netherlands where the company is established.

The output VAT is calculated out of the **margin** made on the total sales price of the travel package minus the third party costs. In turn, the tour operator is not allowed to deduct any input VAT on its purchased services or goods that were bundled into the travel packages, it does have full recovery right on their VAT on overhead costs. As the margin should normally be (local) VAT inclusive, the Dutch output VAT is calculated by the following formula: **21/121 × margin**. In the event that no positive margin is made (i.e. the margin is zero or the travel package is loss making), the reportable output VAT should amount to zero and there should not be a Dutch VAT liability for the travel agent in this respect. In case the Dutch TOMS-regime is applicable, Dutch VAT is calculated out of the (VAT inclusive) margin made either at a rate of 21% or 0%:

* The standard VAT rate of **21%** applies to travel packages that consist of travel services that take place **within the EU**.
* Travel packages consisting of travel services that take place **outside of the EU** should normally be subject to the **zero rate**.
* If the trip consists of parts in the EU and outside of the EU a **pro rata** percentage may need to be applied.

Currently, the Dutch TOMS-rules allow a Dutch tour operator to choose to calculate the margin on the basis of individual trips or on the basis of specified time periods (globalization method). The choice for the calculation method should be identifiable within the administration.

In certain situations where travel services are provided, the Dutch TOMS-regime should **not** apply. Please find below a few examples where the Dutch TOMS-rules should not be applicable:

* Apart from using supplies of services and/or goods from other taxable persons, the travel agent may also provide own supplies of services and/or goods to the customer within the travel package. The Dutch TOMS-regime does not apply to the **own supplies** of services and/or goods. The 'normal' VAT rules apply to these type of supplies.
* Another example of a situation, in which the Dutch TOMS-regime does not apply, is when a taxable person provides only **one travel service** (e.g. a flight) and does not bundle various supplies of services and/or goods from other taxable persons in a travel package, although recent EU-case law seems to indicate that should very well be possible. We will explore this in more detail below.

Latest Dutch Policy Guidelines: Besluit van 19 augustus 2019, nr. 2019-7277, included in the mail with this memo.

**In summary:**

* A Travel package qualifies as one service to the traveler. With regard to packaging: despite the fact that the decree explicitly states that one service element may be sufficient for the application of TOMS, it is nevertheless included that the provision of several travel services is characteristic of services provided by travel agents/tour operators
* Taxable for VAT in the country where the company is located
* VAT due on the profit margin
* No right to deduct VAT for the travel elements
* NL TOMS only applies to travel within the EU

## Other Services Than the Mentioned TOMS-Services

The definition of services in the Directive (art. 24) and the Dutch VAT Act (art. 4) is very broad, namely any supply against remuneration that is not a supply of goods. This can be straight forward, meaning that someone wants a service to be performed, for instance a flight from A to B and pays an amount for this service. Other services can sometimes be less easy to recognize and have wordings as commission, service fee, kick-back, margin etc.

As Michiel van Oss already explained in quite some detail only if C-Teleport acts in its own name and for its own account (as principal), or in its own name, but for the account of the service provider (commissionaire or undisclosed agent) the TOMS rules come into play. If single items are sold as principal or commissionair the VAT treatment follows the initial treatment if these cannot be seen as tour operator services.

All other intermediary type services are for the purpose of this memorandum some form of agency or intermediary or general services. This type of agent is often referred to as the **disclosed agent**, in the sense that that the buyer knows who the services supplier is that performs the underlying services. The (intermediary) services of a disclosed agent are subject to the normal VAT regime.

The travel type services that the agent sells on behalf of the supplier are classified **suspended account type income** in other words the amount received that is forwarded to the service supplier should not be considered as turnover or purchase, but only the difference between the two is the actual remuneration (commission fee). The commission that the agent receives for his (intermediary) service is taxed with VAT, depending on the place of supply.

* In **Business-to-Business** situations, the levying of VAT is shifted to the customer if that customer is based in other EU countries or outside scope if they belong outside the EU. In that case, the agent sends an invoice for his intermediary services with the words "reverse charge VAT or outside scope of VAT" to his customer. Sometimes this can be done by the customer by means of self-billing. **Dutch Customers should be charged local VAT.** Note that specifically intermediary services with regard to airline tickets can benefit from a **0% rate**, which is important for the overall amount of Dutch VAT due in C Teleport's current business.
* In **Business-to-Consumer** situations, the intermediary service is taxed in the EU country where the underlying service is taxable. New rules are currently made where it regards platforms that do this type of service, but as C-Teleport is currently working for Business only we will not cover these new rules here. Of course we can in a later stage discuss this when appropriate.

In this respect I want to stress that depending on the party paying for the fee as a result of a contract the place of supply is determined. This could be the airline, train or hotel operator or the customer (the business purchasing the services for its personnel). As this is rightfully concluded by Michiel van Oss this could mean that Dutch VAT is due (Dutch operators or customers), reverse charged (EU operators/customers) or outside scope non-EU operators. Note that businesses can always deduct this VAT (either charged or due based on the reverse charge mechanism and in that respect the VAT itself does not become a cost) as they need these services for their business.

## Prior VAT Treatment Versions from TaxConnection

You informed me initially that there were two different VAT treatment scenarios that TaxConnection has proposed throughout our engagement regarding our three main business lines: Software as a Service (SaaS), flight tickets, and hotels.

### First Version (Applied for Q3 and Q4 2024)

This was the initial approach suggested by TaxConnection and the one used for the VAT declaration:

* **SaaS:** 21% NL / Reverse charge EU / 0% outside EU
* **Flight tickets:** 0% always, both margin and non-margin
* **Hotels:** 9% NL (only for the margin), 0% for the non-margin part

The declaration included:

1. **1A:** SaaS (NL)
2. **1E:** Ticket margin (NL)
3. **3B:** SaaS and ticket margin (EU) — declared in the ICP too

The SaaS and ticket margin outside the EU were not reported in any box. Additionally, the hotel margin was also omitted, as the approach was unclear at that time.

### Revised Version (Proposed by TaxConnection -- Seeking a Second Opinion)

The latest approach proposed by TaxConnection would have a significant negative impact on C Teleport, as it considers all our margin transactions as a service subject to 21% VAT, regardless of whether we are selling SaaS, flight tickets, or hotels.

1. **SaaS:** 21% NL / Reverse charge EU / 0% outside EU
2. **Flight tickets:** 21% NL / Reverse charge EU / 0% outside EU (margin) and 0% for non-margin
3. **Hotels:** 21% NL / Reverse charge EU / 0% outside EU (margin) and 0% for non-margin

Under this approach, the VAT declaration would include:

1. **1A:** SaaS + margin for flights and hotels (NL)
2. **3B:** SaaS + margin for flights and hotels (EU) — declared in the ICP too

SaaS and ticket margin outside the EU would not be reported in any box.

I believe the main worry with the initial conclusions by TaxConnection is that the flight tickets were always "sold" at 0% and the margin is not always shown towards the customers. And the new suggested model is changing that into a taxation on the margin. Meanwhile it also states that for non-margin 0% should be supplied on both flight tickets and hotels. I am not sure whether I follow this from your contracts. I also not fully understand the outcome of the conclusion "agency" and the suggested model, as it looks like VAT is attached to the location of the underlying supply rather than the place of the customer.

## My Concluding Arguments for Treatment Based on Our Last Discussions

If the supplies are not bundled or combined TOMS should not apply. There is a risk that if customer can easily combine products and one price is paid for the total, that the DTA finds that TOMS could apply. The travel to EU locations could then be subject to Dutch VAT (on the margin). Especially if C Teleport bears risks this could apply, however the current contracts reviewed seem to indicate that C Teleport may only act as intermediary or agent.

**If C Teleport acts as agent the following treatment should apply:**

* **SaaS:** 21% NL / Reverse charge EU / 0% outside EU depending on location customer
* **Flight tickets only:** 0% VAT (note that we have discussed that the current Dutch rules allow for not disclosing the margin). The text in Dutch is: *Het bemiddelen bij het sluiten van overeenkomsten tot het in de post bedoelde vervoer van personen valt ook onder het nultarief. Voor de uitleg van het begrip 'bemiddeling' wordt verwezen naar onderdeel 3.4 van de toelichting bij post b.1. Als de tussenkomst op eigen naam plaatsvindt, dan geldt daarvoor het nultarief op grond van post b.3. Als de bemiddeling op naam en voor rekening van derden plaatsvindt, valt de bemiddelingsdienst op grond van post b.4 onder het nultarief.* Also the site of the DTA currently has the following text: *Als u handelt als tussenpersoon of als u bemiddelt bij het verlenen van een aantal specifieke diensten, past u het 0%-tarief toe. Op eigen naam of voor een ander: Bij een aantal diensten maakt het niet uit of u bij het bemiddelen op eigen naam optreedt of handelt op naam en voor rekening van een ander. * **Hotels (intermediary):** 21% NL on the margin / Reverse charge EU on the margin / 0% outside EU (margin) depending on the place of establishment of the customer, the hotel bill is shown including local VAT of the country where the hotel is located, customer can ask for an invoice in its own name in order to ask for refunds of local EU VAT.

## Conclusion

There is a risk that the work of C Teleport is seen as bundling by the DTA if more than one travel item is provided for in one trip, this is especially tricky where hotels and other travel means than flight tickets are included and C Teleport sets its own prices.

If travel is arranged for to non-EU locations applying TOMS does not lead to the payment of Dutch VAT, so for this type of travel arrangements this might be an opportunity, rather than a risk.

**For intermediary services with regard to flight tickets the current treatment of applying 0% is correct.** As 0% applies on the whole ticket price separately mentioning the margin is not obligatory.

**Intermediary services on other items may require mentioning the margin on the invoice**, applying for instance the reduced Dutch VAT rate of 9% on the total invoices of the Dutch hotels (including your margin) is therefore incorrect. If the original supplier is mentioned in detail (including its local VAT) this may help the position that there is indeed an intermediary service supplied.

Some items mentioned like credit card fees and FX fees may be **exempt financial transactions**, which means that also no VAT should apply on these items. Note that if the turnover on the supplies is more than 1% this may have an impact on your VAT recovery rate of general costs (non-travel related).
